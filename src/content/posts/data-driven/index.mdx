## Background

In June 2024, I joined [Scribe](https://scribe.com) as its first (and only) Design Engineer. Scribe's VP of Design, [Tony Gines](https://www.tonygines.com/), had hired me to take the existing design system and improve its consistency, polish, and accessibility.

Scribe is a tool—commonly used through a [browser extension](https://scribe.com/install/install-extension)—which allows recording clicks and keystrokes to automatically generate step-by-step guides. It's commonly used for creating things like product tutorials and standard operating procedures (SOPs), and is used by over 600,000 businesses, including [94% of the Fortune 500](https://scribe.com/customers).

### The problem

Scribe's design system, called Stylus, was beginning to show cracks. The system was built on a solid foundation using [Radix UI](https://www.radix-ui.com) and [Tailwind](https://tailwindcss.com). But as the company expanded, without a dedicated maintainer, issues had emerged:

- **Fragmented documentation** made it unclear what capabilities the system had and how it was intended to be used
- **Low adoption** meant that teams often defaulted to building new, one-off components, rather than contributing back to the system
- **Inconsistent component APIs** and a lack of strategy around tokens caused inconsistencies that affected the user experience and damaged trust
- **Accessibility issues**—like low-contrast text, missing focus rings, and unclickable checkbox labels—prevented certain users from accessing Scribe altogether

I had built [previous design systems](/posts/commonplace) and I knew how to address these problems. But I wanted to go a step further and quantify design system improvements over time. I wanted a holistic image of what was working and what was not.

## Taking a pulse: design system surveys

The month I joined, I wrote and sent a survey sent to every engineer, designer, and product manager. I would send this same survey every six months during my tenure.

Each survey asked participants to rate six statements—feature coverage, predictability, naming, time savings, documentation, and cross-team collaboration—on a 5-point [Likert scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likert_scale), plus an overall quality score, on a 10-point scale. Low ratings prompted open-ended feedback that fed directly into design system priorities.

You can see upward trends across nearly all categories.

The share of people **using existing components for most new features increased by 10%** thanks to improved teamwide communication about design system updates, better documentation, and preemptive updates to support new product features (for example, by updating a suite of components to support dark mode before the launch of a new product feature that prominently used dark colors).

**12% more people reported that components work the way they expect**, and **11% more said names were understandable** thanks to feedback (often received via the survey!) that was then integrated into the system. Duplicate configurations were eliminated and streamlined; shared types were extracted; patterns were established for naming props. This all made the system easier to use.

The **ability to answer questions via documentation rose by 40%** thanks to investment in writing centralized, reader-friendly docs with [Starlight](https://starlight.astro.build). (The new docs, written as Markdown located within our monorepo, made it easy to keep up-to-date and easy to access for agentic tools like Claude.) Documentation drift was minimized by automatically populating API reference tables based on JSDoc comments and TypeScript types.


<RatingOverall />

I supplemented the sentiment-driven survey data with quantitative data from [Omlet](https://omlet.dev), a tool which runs static analysis on a codebase to determine how many times a component is used, how many times a prop is used, and chart coverage over time. Thanks to insights from Omlet and a lot of deleted code, **component coverage increased from 37% to 52% over 6 months**.

## Reflections

Building a design system is a perpetual fight against entropy—the tendency of any sufficiently complex system to devolve into chaos. Taking a regular pulse helps the team stay focused on building what needs building. A few recommendations:

- **Invest in documentation.** There should be one source of truth. It should be easy to access and it should be easy to update. (Yes, even in the age of LLMs! Design system decisions—particularly ones around patterns which can't be directly represented in code—need to be written down for an LLM to know about them.)
- **Communicate!** Post updates, celebrate contributions, and respond quickly to fixes and feedback. If someone contributes a fix or suggests a change to the design system, thank them! This builds momentum for future improvements.
- **Measure more than just sentiment.** Survey feedback is important, but it skews toward developer experience over end user experience. Make sure you supplement internal surveys with performance testing, accessibility testing, and visual regression testing using tools like [Chromatic](https://www.chromatic.com), [Playwright](https://playwright.dev), and [axe-core](https://github.com/dequelabs/axe-core).
